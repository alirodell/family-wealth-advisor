# Tier 4: Peak Accumulator

## Defining markers
- Top federal marginal bracket territory (37%, or close to it)
- All Tier 3 tax-advantaged space (backdoor Roth, SEP/Solo 401(k) or 401(k) max) is typically already in use
- Meaningful surplus cash flow beyond what standard tax-advantaged accounts can absorb
- Illustrative range: ~$450k–$1M+ household income (confirm current-year top-bracket threshold in REFERENCE.md)

## Account priority order
1. **Employer 401(k) match and max**, HSA max, backdoor Roth for both spouses, SEP-IRA/Solo 401(k) if self-employed — all Tier 3 vehicles remain the foundation and should already be fully used before anything below
2. **Mega-backdoor Roth**, if the 401(k) plan allows after-tax contributions with in-plan Roth conversion or in-service withdrawal to a Roth IRA — confirm with the plan administrator; this can unlock tens of thousands of dollars per year of additional Roth space, often the largest untapped lever at this tier
3. **S-corp election evaluation**, for self-employed clients with substantial net income — paying a reasonable W-2 salary and taking the remainder as a distribution (not subject to self-employment tax) is standard practice at this income level, but the "reasonable compensation" determination needs a CPA
4. **Whole life insurance evaluation** — worth a serious conversation once age (35–50), income ($300k+), and bracket (32%+) line up *and* standard tax-advantaged accounts are already maxed and there's a genuine permanent (not just temporary) death-benefit need. See the Whole Life Insurance Decision Framework in SKILL.md for the full criteria, including the 7-pay test, MEC status, and PUA rider mechanics. Sequence matters: term life for the temporary need, tax-advantaged accounts optimized, whole life only after — not a first move, and always flag independent CPA/CFP review before purchase
5. **Municipal bonds for the fixed-income sleeve of taxable holdings** — at this bracket, the tax-exempt yield on munis (especially in-state munis, which can be triple-tax-exempt in high-tax states) often beats taxable bond yields net of tax
6. **Umbrella liability insurance**, sized to net worth — becomes increasingly important as assets accumulate and liability exposure grows
7. **Tax-location strategy across taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth accounts** — deliberate placement of tax-inefficient assets (bonds, REITs) in tax-advantaged accounts and tax-efficient assets (qualified-dividend equities) in taxable becomes worth real money at this bracket

## Not yet relevant (and why)
- **Advanced estate structures** (SLATs, ILITs, GRATs, dynasty trusts) — evaluate based on net worth relative to estate-tax exemption thresholds (see Tier 5), not automatically at this income level; a high earner early in their career may not be there yet even at this income
- Note that Tier 4 income and Tier 5 net worth commonly arrive together over time, but not always — check net worth explicitly rather than assuming

## Common pitfalls at this tier
- Buying whole life insurance before confirming standard tax-advantaged accounts are actually maxed, or without a genuine permanent need — this is the single most common point where whole life gets oversold, since the household now plausibly fits the demographic even when the specific policy doesn't fit the need
- Not checking whether the 401(k) plan supports mega-backdoor Roth before assuming it's unavailable
- Treating estate planning as automatic at this income level rather than checking actual net worth against exemption thresholds
- Letting a large taxable brokerage balance sit without tax-location optimization, given how much bracket and NIIT exposure now compound the cost of poor asset placement

## Graduation signal
Net worth (not income) approaching federal or state estate-tax exemption thresholds — this triggers Tier 5 considerations *alongside* continued Tier 4 accumulation, not as a replacement for it.
