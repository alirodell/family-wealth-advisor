# Tier 3: High Earner / Phase-Out Zone

## Defining markers
- Household MAGI above the Roth IRA phase-out threshold — direct Roth contributions are no longer available to one or both spouses
- Household income above the Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) threshold (a fixed, non-inflation-indexed $250k MFJ / $200k single) — an additional 3.8% tax on net investment income now applies
- Marginal federal bracket typically 32%–35%
- If self-employed: approaching or inside the Section 199A Qualified Business Income (QBI) phase-out band for specified service trades or businesses (SSTBs) — consulting, law, medicine, financial services, and similar fields
- Illustrative range: ~$250k–$450k household income (confirm current-year phase-out and bracket thresholds in REFERENCE.md)

## Account priority order
1. **Employer 401(k) match**, in full, always first
2. **HSA max**, if HDHP-eligible
3. **Backdoor Roth IRA for both spouses** — the mechanism: a non-deductible traditional IRA contribution followed immediately by a Roth conversion. Before executing, always check for existing pre-tax traditional/SEP/SIMPLE IRA balances — the pro-rata rule (full mechanics in REFERENCE.md) means an existing balance makes the conversion partially taxable, not clean
4. **SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k)**, if either spouse has self-employment income — Solo 401(k) is often preferable to SEP-IRA specifically because SEP-IRA balances count toward the pro-rata calculation and can block a clean backdoor Roth, while 401(k)-type balances do not
5. **401(k) contributions maxed** for any W-2 income
6. **529 contributions**, at the level that captures any state deduction, balanced against the fact that retirement savings should not be shortchanged for education savings
7. **Taxable brokerage**, with attention to tax location — given NIIT exposure and a higher bracket, asset location (which holdings sit in taxable vs. tax-deferred vs. Roth) starts to matter more than at earlier tiers

## Not yet relevant (and why)
- **Whole life insurance** — the sweet spot for whole life starts at Tier 4 (age 35–50, income $300k+, bracket 32%+, tax-advantaged accounts already maxed); a household at the lower end of Tier 3 that hasn't maxed backdoor Roth and SEP/Solo 401(k) yet isn't there
- **Mega-backdoor Roth** — typically requires a 401(k) plan that allows after-tax contributions with in-plan conversion; worth checking plan documents, but this is more commonly relevant once Tier 3's other tax-advantaged space is already fully used, which tends to coincide with Tier 4 income
- **S-corp election** — self-employment tax savings from an S-corp election become material as net self-employment income grows; evaluate this as the household approaches Tier 4, not automatically at the start of Tier 3
- **Advanced estate structures** — evaluate independently based on net worth (see Tier 5), not automatically at this income level

## Common pitfalls at this tier
- Executing a backdoor Roth without checking for existing pre-tax IRA balances first, then being surprised by a partially taxable conversion under the pro-rata rule
- Not flagging the SEP-IRA vs. Solo 401(k) choice to a newly self-employed client — defaulting to SEP-IRA (the more commonly known vehicle) can quietly block future backdoor Roth conversions
- Ignoring NIIT exposure when locating assets between taxable and tax-advantaged accounts
- For SSTB business owners, not flagging the QBI phase-out — the 20% deduction shrinks and disappears through this income band, and additional pre-tax retirement contributions can partially offset that by reducing taxable income

## Graduation signal
Top federal bracket territory, or tax-advantaged space (backdoor Roth, SEP/Solo 401(k), 401(k) max) is fully used with meaningful surplus cash flow remaining — move to Tier 4, where whole life insurance evaluation, mega-backdoor Roth, and S-corp election become live questions.
